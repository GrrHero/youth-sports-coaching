# Week 4 Home Training Plan: "Aim It, Fake It, Lead It"
**Week of Monday, Oct 26, 2026 · For Greg's boys (7 & 5)**

## 1. Week Overview

**Theme: Play on purpose.** Last week the boys learned to take the ball in and send it somewhere. This week they decide *where* and *when*: hit the ball to a chosen part of the field, field a grounder and fire it to first before the runner gets there, fake out a defender, throw to where a receiver is *going*, and react fast from awkward starting positions. Every activity has a direction, a target or an opponent to read. That's the step from "doing the move" to "playing the game."

**What carries over from Week 3:**

| Week 3 skill | Week 4 next step |
|---|---|
| Tee and soft-toss swings (Weeks 1–2), target throws | **Front-toss hitting aimed at a gate**: up the middle (both), opposite field (7yo) |
| Alligator grounders + crow hop target throws (two separate drills) | **Field-and-fire**: one smooth move from the grounder to a throw at first, then a race against a runner |
| First touch, shielding (keeping the ball) | **1v1 moves**: a fake shot and a cut-away to *beat* a defender, not just keep the ball |
| Phone throws at a basket, routes (Week 2) | **Lead passes** to a moving receiver at a landing spot |
| Moonwalk backpedal | **Backpedal, read the QB, break on the ball** |
| Whisper landings, skater bounds, Pounce reactions | **Single-leg hops, hopscotch patterns**, and **reaction starts** from lying, sitting and facing backward |

**Suggested schedule** (move days around as needed; 20 to 30 minutes each):
- **Mon 10/26:** Session 1, Baseball: Aim the Swing & Field-and-Fire
- **Tue 10/27:** Session 2, Soccer: Fake It, Slice It, Beat the Troll
- **Thu 10/29:** Session 3, Flag Football: Lead the Receiver & Break on the Ball
- **Fri 10/30:** Session 4, Athleticism: Hop, Pattern, Blast Off
- **Sat or Sun 10/31–11/1:** Session 5, Mixed Backyard Day: Halloween Haunted Gauntlet (Saturday is Halloween; a morning session burns off energy before trick-or-treating, or save it for Sunday)

**Three rules for the whole week:**
1. **Target first, technique second.** Every activity has a gate, a spot or a defender. Point at the target instead of fixing body parts. Kids fix a lot of their own technique when the goal is clear.
2. **One cue word per activity.** Same as before: only that word while they're moving. If you catch yourself saying a sentence, stop and say the word.
3. **One level each.** Each session lists one clear level for the 5yo and one for the 7yo. Start them there. Move up only after about 4 successes out of 5; move down after 3 misses in a row. No discussion, just quietly change the distance or speed.

**How to teach this week (the 4-step loop).** Teaching feels harder than playing because when you play, your body does the work; when you teach, you have to slow down and *show* what your body already knows. Use the same loop for every activity:
1. **Show** (silent demo, 15 seconds): slow, exaggerated, 2 or 3 times, then once at normal speed.
2. **Go** (within 30 seconds of starting): they try it right away. The first reps will be messy. That's the point; you're looking for what to fix.
3. **Cue** (one word, while they move): say the cue *before* the rep, not after a miss. "Laser!" before the toss is coaching; "You didn't hit it at the gate" after is a lecture.
4. **Ask** (at a water break): "What made that one go straight?" Let them answer. What they say in their own words is what they'll remember.

**New this week, the "change one thing" rule:** when something isn't working, change *one* thing about the setup (distance, ball size, speed, target size) instead of explaining more. If the 5yo keeps missing, move closer or use a bigger ball. If the 7yo is bored, make the target smaller or add a defender. Adjusting the task is almost always faster than adjusting the kid.

## 2. Session 1: Baseball: Aim the Swing & Field-and-Fire (28 min)

**Equipment:** 2 gloves, 1 bat per boy (or a light plastic bat for the 5yo), 10 to 12 wiffle balls or foam balls (no hard balls for front toss), 6 cones (or shoes), a laundry basket and a lawn chair, a base or towel for first base, a bucket to sit on.

**Levels this session:**
- **5yo:** Front toss from 8 to 10 feet with a big wiffle ball, aiming only up the middle. Fields slow rolls straight at him, stands up, steps and throws into a basket 15 feet away (no hop).
- **7yo:** Front toss from 12 to 15 feet with a regular wiffle ball, up the middle then opposite field. Fields grounders one step to either side and crow-hops a throw to first 30 to 40 feet away.

> **Safety note for you:** front toss means you're in front of the hitter. Use **only wiffle or foam balls**, kneel or sit on a bucket a **big step off the straight line** toward the hitter's pull side (for a right-handed hitter, that's your right as you face him), toss underhand, then turn your shoulder away. Wear a ball cap. If you own a pop-up net, kneel behind it. Use the Week 3 "Bat Bubble" rule: the waiting brother stands behind a cone 3 big steps back and off to the side.

### Warm-up: Shadow Infield (4 min) · Cue: **Shadow**
- **Setup:** Open grass. Both boys face you, about 5 steps away, gloves on. No ball.
- **Steps:**
  1. You are the leader; they are your shadows and copy everything you do, at the same time.
  2. Do slow-motion moves: shuffle left 2 steps, field an imaginary grounder, bring it to your belly, hop and throw to an imaginary first base.
  3. Mix in arm circles, trunk twists and a few dry throws (big, slow motion).
  4. Speed up a little each round. 6 to 8 sequences.
  5. *7yo add-on:* for the last 2 rounds he becomes the leader and you and his brother are the shadows.
- **Silent demo:** The warm-up *is* the demo. Raise your hand like "watch me," then start moving slowly. Exaggerate everything (huge shuffle, giant throw) so it's easy to copy.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Watches you instead of moving. **Fix:** Go slower and make it silly (a wobbly "slow-motion robot" throw). He'll join when it looks like a game. Stand closer so he can see you better.
  - *5yo:* Copies the wrong side (mirror vs. same side). **Fix:** Totally fine. Don't correct it at 5. The movement pattern matters, not left vs. right.
  - *7yo:* Rushes ahead of you. **Fix:** "Shadows can't move before the person." Freeze mid-move a few times; whoever keeps moving is "a broken shadow" and owes a jumping jack.
  - *7yo:* As leader, does moves too hard for his brother. **Fix:** "Leaders make moves the whole team can do." That's the same leadership rule you'll use at the fields.
- **Make it fun:** Do it to music. Throw in a surprise freeze or a goofy move (a slow-motion dive) every few sequences.
- **Why it works:** It warms up the shoulders and legs and **pre-teaches today's main move** (field, gather, hop, throw) before a real ball shows up. Copying a model with no words is **observational learning**, and kids this age are very good at it. You also get a preview of who struggles with which part before the real drill starts.

### Skill Work A: Laser Gate Front Toss (8 min) · Cue: **Laser**
- **Setup:** Hitter at a home plate (towel or base). Two cones about 8 big steps apart, 30 to 40 feet out, straight up the middle behind you: the "Laser Gate." For the 7yo, add a second gate on the **opposite field side** (right field for a right-handed hitter). You kneel or sit 8 to 10 feet (5yo) or 12 to 15 feet (7yo) away, off the line as in the safety note.
- **Steps:**
  1. Hitter in his normal stance from Weeks 1–2.
  2. You toss underhand: a soft, flat toss into the middle of the strike zone (belly-button height).
  3. He tries to hit a **line drive through the Laser Gate**, "back where the ball came from."
  4. Through the gate on a line or a hard bounce = 2 points. Hit but outside the gate = 1. Miss = 0, and nobody comments.
  5. Rounds of 6 swings, then swap brothers.
  6. *7yo progression:* after 2 rounds up the middle, toss slightly to the **outside half** of the plate and point at the opposite-field gate. He tries to send it there. Then mix: you point to a gate before each toss.
- **Silent demo:** Have the 7yo toss you one (from close, softly). Before the toss, point at the gate with your bat, then sight down your arm like aiming a laser. Swing level and freeze your finish with your belly button and chest facing the gate. Point at the gate again and nod. Twice.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Swings way too early or late at the moving ball. **Fix:** Move closer and toss slower and higher (a rainbow toss). Say "Laser" as your hand starts to swing back, not as the ball arrives. That gives him a rhythm to start his swing.
  - *5yo:* Takes his eyes off the ball to look at the gate. **Fix:** "Look at the gate *before* the toss, then only the ball." Have him point at the gate, then point at your tossing hand.
  - *7yo:* Pulls everything foul or to the pull side ("rolling over"). **Fix:** Don't explain wrists. Move the toss a few inches to the outside half and move his gate. The target changes the swing for you. Ask: "Where was the ball when you hit it, out in front or deeper?"
  - *7yo:* Uppercuts and pops everything up. **Fix:** "Lasers don't go to the moon." Any ball higher than a basketball hoop = 0 points. The scoring fixes it.
- **Make it fun:** He's a laser cannon. Do sound effects ("pew!") for every gate hit. Keep a running score per round, and let each boy chase his own best round, not his brother's.
- **Why it works:** Aiming at a gate is an **external focus**. He thinks about where the ball goes, and his body sorts out the timing and bat path. Motor-learning research shows that works better than body instructions like "keep your hands inside the ball." Letting him find out that a deeper contact point sends the ball the other way is **guided discovery**: you set up the toss and the gate, he solves it. The "no moonballs" scoring is a **constraint** that rewards line drives without a lecture.
- 🎥 **Video:** [Ripken front toss tip](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Re0coPbz55M) · *Verified title: "Ripken Baseball Hitting Tip - Basic Front Toss" (Ripken Baseball).* Bill Ripken runs front toss to the outside half and has a young hitter drive it back up the middle, which is exactly this drill's goal. Watch it yourself first.

### Skill Work B: Field-and-Fire to First (7 min) · Cue: **Tick-tock**
- **Setup:** "First base" (a towel or base) with a laundry basket propped on a lawn chair as the first baseman (or the brother with his glove standing on the base). The fielder starts at a shortstop-ish spot: 15 feet from the target (5yo) or 30 to 40 feet (7yo). You roll from home plate.
- **Steps:**
  1. Fielder in ready position.
  2. You roll a grounder. He fields it out in front with two hands, alligator style, and brings it up to his belly: that's **"Tick."**
  3. **"Tock"**: he turns his glove-side shoulder toward first and throws.
     - *5yo:* stand up, side-step toward first, throw into the basket. No hop.
     - *7yo:* crow hop toward first (the little hop from last week), throw, finish with his hand by his opposite pocket.
  4. Say the rhythm out loud together: "Tick" (fielded), "tock" (thrown). No long pause between.
  5. 6 to 8 reps each, alternating brothers. The resting brother can be the first baseman.
- **Silent demo:** Have a boy roll you a grounder. Field it, pause at your belly with a big head-nod ("tick"), then turn, crow-hop to first and throw ("tock"), freezing in your finish. Do it in slow motion twice with your head nodding on each beat, then once at full speed.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Throws from wherever he fields it, while still bent over. **Fix:** "Stand tall, then tock." Let him take his time between tick and tock at first. Speed comes later.
  - *5yo:* Throws back toward you (the roller) instead of first. **Fix:** Put a big, silly object (a stuffed animal or bright towel) in the basket: "Feed the bear at first base." Point to it before every roll.
  - *7yo:* Hops before he has the ball (rushing). **Fix:** "No tock without a tick." A throw before a clean field doesn't count. Roll a bit slower until his hands finish first.
  - *7yo:* Crow hops toward home, not first, so his throw sails. **Fix:** Lay a jump rope on the ground from his fielding spot toward first: "Hop along the rope." The line shows the direction without words.
- **Make it fun:** Count "outs." Ball in the basket or caught by the brother = out. Three outs = "inning over" and he gets a victory lap around the bases.
- **Why it works:** Last week the grounder and the throw were taught as two separate drills (**part practice**). Now they're joined into one move (**whole practice**). The "tick-tock" rhythm gives the timing a sound, and rhythm cues help kids link two movements into one smooth action. A real first base (even a basket) gives the throw a purpose.
- 🎥 **Video:** [Crow hop throwing progression](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PfmEL02N0ns) · *Verified title: "Crow Hop + Throwing Progression Drill" (MegRem Softball).* A step-by-step progression from the crow hop into the throw. It's softball, but the footwork is the same. For you, not the boys.

### Game: Beat the Runner (6 min) · Cue: **Race**
- **Setup:** Same field. You roll from home, then *you* are the runner. First base target as before (basket on the chair or the brother on the base).
- **Steps:**
  1. You roll a grounder to the fielder, then immediately run toward first.
  2. He fields (tick) and throws to first (tock).
  3. Ball in the basket or caught at first before you touch the base = **OUT**, point for him. You get there first = **SAFE**, point for you.
  4. First to 5. Then the brother fields.
  5. *5yo:* you run backward or hop on one foot, slowly and dramatically. *7yo:* you jog for real, and speed up as he gets better.
- **Silent demo:** Have the 7yo roll and run while you field. Field, throw, then point at first base and give a big umpire "OUT!" signal (fist pump). Then point at the runner and at yourself, like "it's you against me."
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Panics and throws before he has the ball. **Fix:** Slow your run way down. "The runner is a turtle today." Let him win the first few clearly.
  - *5yo:* Chases you with the ball to tag you. **Fix:** Laugh. It's a smart idea, just not today's rule. "Throw it to the bear; the bear makes the out."
  - *7yo:* Rushes and the throw goes wild. **Fix:** Ask, don't tell: "What happened on that throw?" He'll often say "I hurried." Then: "Was there time to tick?" Adjust your run so there's just enough time.
  - *7yo:* Argues safe/out calls. **Fix:** Let the brother be the umpire on close plays, and accept the call no matter what. That's a good lesson in itself.
- **Make it fun:** Narrate like a radio announcer: "Grounder to short... he fields... the throw... OUT! The crowd goes wild!" Do dramatic slides into first when you're safe.
- **Why it works:** A runner adds **real game pressure** and a reason to be quick *and* accurate. He learns the trade-off himself: too slow and the runner's safe, too rushed and the throw misses. That's **decision-making in context**, which practice without a runner can't teach. Adjusting your speed keeps his win rate around 60 to 70%, the sweet spot for effort and fun.

### Cool-down: Pancake Stretch (3 min) · Cue: **Pancake**
- **Setup:** Sit on the grass with legs straight out in front, a ball in hand.
- **Steps:**
  1. Slowly roll the ball from the knees down to the toes and back, "flattening the pancake batter." 5 slow rolls.
  2. Butterfly sit (soles of the feet together) and let the knees drop gently like a pancake on the griddle. 3 slow breaths.
  3. Lie back flat as a pancake for 3 breaths.
  4. Question: "Which gate did you hit the most, and what did you do on those swings?"
- **Silent demo:** Sit down, roll the ball slowly down your legs with a "mmm, pancakes" face, then flop flat on your back.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Bounces his knees fast. **Fix:** "Slow pancake, or it burns!" Rest your hands lightly on his knees to slow the rhythm.
  - *7yo:* Rushes to finish. **Fix:** "The pancake isn't cooked until 3 slow breaths." Count them out loud.
- **Make it fun:** Ask what kind of pancake they are today (chocolate chip, blueberry, "home run pancake").
- **Why it works:** Gentle hamstring and hip stretches after squatting and throwing, plus slow breathing to settle down. The question asks *what they did* on good swings, which links the result to the action in their own words.

## 3. Session 2: Soccer: Fake It, Slice It, Beat the Troll (26 min)

**Equipment:** 2 soccer balls (size 3), 8 to 10 cones, a small goal (pop-up goal or two shoes as posts).

**Levels this session:**
- **5yo:** Fakes and slices against a **cone defender** at walking-to-jogging speed. Plays the 1v1 game against you in "slow troll" mode.
- **7yo:** Fakes and slices against a **live defender** (you) who reacts only to a convincing fake. Plays the 1v1 game at full speed, and as the troll against his brother with handicap rules.

### Warm-up: Bumper Car Dribble (4 min) · Cue: **Heads-up**
- **Setup:** A cone square about 12 x 12 big steps. Each boy has a ball. You walk around inside the square without a ball as a "bumper car."
- **Steps:**
  1. Boys dribble anywhere in the square with little touches.
  2. Their ball must not touch you or each other.
  3. Every few seconds, hold up some fingers. Whoever shouts the number first gets a point. (They can only see your fingers if their head is up.)
  4. 3 rounds of about 60 seconds.
  5. *7yo add-on:* he has to call the number *and* change direction.
- **Silent demo:** Dribble slowly inside the square with your head up, swiveling it left and right like a periscope, glancing down at the ball only now and then. Hold up three fingers to yourself and mouth "three!" Then point to your eyes and up.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Stares at the ball and never sees your fingers. **Fix:** Hold your hand low (at his eye level) and close at first. Use big signals like a wave or a silly face instead of numbers.
  - *5yo:* Kicks the ball too far and chases it. **Fix:** Shrink the square. "Keep your ball close enough to tickle it."
  - *7yo:* Calls numbers but stops dribbling to look. **Fix:** Only numbers called *while moving* count.
  - *7yo:* Goes too fast and crashes. **Fix:** "Bumper cars get a ticket for crashing." A crash = a point for you.
- **Make it fun:** Make car horn noises ("beep beep!") and drive around slowly, sometimes "breaking down" in their path.
- **Why it works:** Beating a defender starts with seeing him. The finger-count game makes **scanning** (lifting the head while dribbling) a habit without ever saying "keep your head up." It also warms up ball control with lots of touches.

### Skill Work A: The Pretend Shot (6 min) · Cue: **Trick**
- **Setup:** A cone 10 steps from the start line as the "defender" (5yo), or you standing there (7yo). A small goal 5 steps behind the defender.
- **Steps:**
  1. Dribble toward the defender.
  2. About 2 big steps away, **swing the kicking leg like a real shot**: arms up, big wind-up.
  3. Instead of kicking, the foot **stops just over (or beside) the ball**.
  4. Then use the **inside of the same foot** to push the ball sideways past the defender (soccer coaches call this "fake and take").
  5. Two quick touches forward and shoot into the goal.
  6. 6 to 8 reps, switching feet after the first 4.
  7. *5yo:* cone defender, walking speed. His "fake" can be just lifting the leg big and freezing, then tapping the ball sideways with any part of the foot.
  8. *7yo:* you are the defender. At first you react dramatically to every fake (jump, cover your face) and he slips past. After a few reps, react only if the fake is believable. That's the real test.
- **Silent demo:** Dribble toward a cone, wind up a huge shot with your arms up and a grimace, freeze the foot over the ball, then look sneaky and tap the ball sideways past the cone. Make a "ta-da" face. Slow motion twice, then at speed.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Actually kicks the ball. **Fix:** Fine; it means he committed. "A trick shot is pretend. Your foot flies over the ball like a bird." Practice the fake on a still ball first.
  - *5yo:* Does the fake right on top of the cone and runs into it. **Fix:** Put a "trick spot" (a shoe or a leaf) 2 steps before the cone: "Do the trick at the spot."
  - *7yo:* Small, half-hearted fake, so you don't bite. **Fix:** Don't react. Ask: "Did that trick me?" He'll go bigger. Arms up sells the shot.
  - *7yo:* After the fake, pushes the ball straight into your feet. **Fix:** "After the trick, go where I'm *not*." Point to the open side after his fake for a few reps.
- **Make it fun:** Award "Oscar points" for the most dramatic, convincing fake. When you're fooled, get fooled in hilarious ways (fall over, spin around).
- **Why it works:** The fake shot is the easiest real 1v1 move. It uses a motion they already own (last week's shoelace shot) and just stops it halfway. **Building on a known skill** is faster than teaching a brand-new one. A **live defender who only reacts to a good fake** gives instant, honest feedback no verbal coaching can match.
- 🎥 **Video:** [Fake & Take and other moves](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b2HBI-X_ez4) · *Verified title: "3 Essential Soccer Moves. Fake & Take, Pullbacks & Roll to Hide ⚽" (West Loop Soccer Club).* Shows the fake-and-take used here plus two simple moves you can add in later weeks.

### Skill Work B: Slice-Away Escape (6 min) · Cue: **Slice**
- **Setup:** A "lava line" of 3 cones about 8 steps from the start. Two "safe gates" (2 cones each), one to the left and one to the right, about 4 steps to the side of the lava line.
- **Steps:**
  1. Dribble at the lava line.
  2. Just before it, use the **inside of the foot** to cut the ball sharply sideways or back, a sharp "slice" like cutting a pizza.
  3. Then speed away with 2 or 3 bigger touches through a safe gate.
  4. *5yo:* walking pace, one slice turning back toward the start, then dribble home. Either foot.
  5. *7yo:* jogging pace. You call "Left!" or "Right!" as he approaches, and he slices toward that gate. Then you stand at the line and chase him after his cut.
- **Silent demo:** Dribble toward the line, plant your standing foot and swing the inside of your other foot across the ball to slice it sharply sideways. Explode away with fast touches and pumping arms. Then freeze and draw a sharp "V" in the air with your finger to show the angle.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Makes a wide, slow U-turn instead of a cut. **Fix:** "Pizza slices are sharp." Put a cone right at the turning spot; the ball has to change direction before touching it.
  - *5yo:* Loses the ball after cutting. **Fix:** Slower approach, one touch at a time. Celebrate any turn where the ball stays close.
  - *7yo:* Cuts but doesn't speed up after, so you catch him. **Fix:** Say nothing; just chase him after the cut. He'll quickly learn that the burst is part of the move.
  - *7yo:* Always cuts to his strong side. **Fix:** Call the weaker side more often. Weak-foot slices are worth 2 points.
- **Make it fun:** The line is lava. Slice before the lava and escape to a safe gate. Count escapes in a row.
- **Why it works:** The cut-away (coaches call it an inside cut) is a **change of direction plus a change of speed**, and that beats defenders more often than fancy tricks. Calling the direction late (7yo) makes him **read and react** rather than run a memorized pattern. The lava line is a visual **constraint** that sets the timing for him.
- 🎥 **Video:** [Inside cut for young players](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=60nDcFWaa8I) · *Verified title: "Inside Cut - 1v1 Moves - Change of Direction - Crossing - Soccer (Football) Skills for U8-U9" (Ultimate Soccer Moves Collection).* A clear demo of the inside cut at a level close to your 7yo's.

### Game: Bridge Troll 1v1 (7 min) · Cue: **Sneak**
- **Setup:** A "bridge" channel 5 steps wide and 12 steps long, marked with cones. A middle line across it where the troll lives. A finish line at the far end.
- **Steps:**
  1. The troll (you at first) guards the middle line and can move only along it, side to side.
  2. The dribbler tries to **sneak past** using the Trick or the Slice, then dribbles to the finish line and stops the ball on it with the sole of his foot.
  3. Made it = 1 point. Troll steals it or knocks it off the bridge = troll's point.
  4. 5 tries each, then switch.
  5. *Brother vs. brother:* when the 7yo is the troll against his brother, he keeps his hands on his head and can only shuffle along the line. When the 5yo is the troll, he can move anywhere on the bridge.
  6. *7yo vs. you:* after a few rounds, you're allowed to leave the line and chase for 3 steps.
- **Silent demo:** Be the dribbler while the 7yo is the troll. Approach slowly, do a big Trick fake, slip past and stop the ball on the finish line with your sole like planting a flag. Then point to the troll's line to show where he can move.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Kicks the ball past the troll and runs after it. **Fix:** That's actually a real move (push and run). Allow it, but the ball has to stay on the bridge.
  - *5yo:* Freezes in front of the troll. **Fix:** Slow troll mode: move in slow motion and roar softly. Say the cue before he gets close: "Sneak!"
  - *7yo:* Tries the same move every time and gets stopped. **Fix:** Ask: "The troll knows that move now. What else have you got?" Let him figure out the switch.
  - *7yo:* Gets frustrated when the troll wins. **Fix:** Give him a "golden ticket": once per round he can call "Freeze, troll!" for 2 seconds.
- **Make it fun:** Big troll voice ("Who's that dribbling over my bridge?"). The troll "eats" stolen balls with dramatic munching.
- **Why it works:** This is a **small-sided game**, the purest way to learn 1v1. The narrow bridge forces him to beat the troll instead of running around him, so the moves from Skill Work A and B become useful, not just practice. The handicaps keep the brother matchup fair, and that's the same tool you'll use at the fields this week (see Section 7).

### Cool-down: Lighthouse Stretch (3 min) · Cue: **Lighthouse**
- **Setup:** Standing, feet wide, holding a soccer ball overhead with both hands.
- **Steps:**
  1. Slowly bend to one side with the ball high, like a lighthouse beam sweeping the sea. Hold 3 seconds. Then the other side.
  2. 4 slow sweeps each way.
  3. Sit and slowly roll the ball in a circle around your body.
  4. Question: "Which move tricked the troll the most?"
- **Silent demo:** Hold the ball high and sweep slowly side to side while making a soft "whoooo" foghorn noise.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Drops the ball or swings it fast. **Fix:** "Lighthouses are slow and strong." Do it beside him at his speed.
  - *7yo:* Turns it into goofing around. **Fix:** "Can you hold the beam perfectly still for 5 seconds?"
- **Make it fun:** Tell a tiny story: "A ship is lost in the fog... sweep the light... there it is!"
- **Why it works:** Side bends stretch the trunk and hips after lots of cutting, and the slow movement brings the heart rate down. The question gets them to name a move that worked, which helps it stick.

## 4. Session 3: Flag Football: Lead the Receiver & Break on the Ball (27 min)

**Equipment:** Flag belts (or rags tucked in waistbands), 1 junior or foam football (foam for the 5yo), 6 to 8 cones, 2 hula hoops (or towels) as landing spots.

**Levels this session:**
- **5yo:** Throws a foam ball to a hoop about 4 steps away while you **walk** slowly toward it across his front. As a defender, walks backward and breaks on a ball you lob gently to one side.
- **7yo:** Throws to a landing spot ahead of a **jogging** receiver on a slant, releasing before the receiver gets there. As a defender, backpedals, reads your throwing arm and breaks on a real throw (with pump fakes).

### Warm-up: Conveyor Belt Catches (5 min) · Cue: **Reach**
- **Setup:** Two cones 8 steps apart in a line, side-on to you. You stand 3 steps (5yo) or 5 steps (7yo) in front of the line with the foam football.
- **Steps:**
  1. The kid walks (5yo) or jogs (7yo) from one cone to the other, crossing in front of you like on a conveyor belt.
  2. You toss the ball **slightly ahead** of him.
  3. He **reaches** his hands out in front to catch it (hands out toward the ball, not trapping it against the chest), then tucks it.
  4. Back the other way. 8 to 10 catches each.
- **Silent demo:** Have the 7yo toss to you while you jog across. Reach both hands far out in front, eyes on the ball, catch it with your fingers, then tuck. Hold the reach pose and wiggle your fingers.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Stops walking to catch. **Fix:** Fine at first; toss to where he is. Then "keep walking, the conveyor belt never stops." Walk beside him holding his hand while his brother tosses.
  - *5yo:* Traps the ball against his chest. **Fix:** Use the softest, biggest ball you have and toss it to his hands, not his chest.
  - *7yo:* Slows down before the ball arrives. **Fix:** Toss a bit farther ahead. The only way to get it is to keep running.
  - *7yo:* Catches, then forgets to tuck. **Fix:** "Reach, then lock it away." Give a gentle swipe at the ball after the catch to test.
- **Make it fun:** You're the factory and the balls are "packages." Count packages delivered. A dropped one "broke," so make a silly crash noise.
- **Why it works:** Catching on the move is new for them. Short, soft tosses at walking speed are an **easy-to-hard progression**, and it warms up the receiver side of today's main skill, so lead passes have someone ready to catch them.

### Skill Work A: Landing-Zone Lead Passes (7 min) · Cue: **Ahead**
- **Setup:** A QB spot (cone). A hula hoop or towel on the ground as the landing zone: 4 steps away for the 5yo, 7 to 8 steps away for the 7yo. The receiver (you or the brother) starts 5 steps to the side of the hoop.
- **Steps:**
  1. On "Hut!", the receiver moves toward the landing zone.
     - *5yo throwing:* you walk slowly across toward the hoop.
     - *7yo throwing:* you or his brother jog a **slant** (3 steps forward, then angle across toward the hoop).
  2. The QB throws the ball **to the hoop, not to the receiver**. The ball should arrive at about the same time as the receiver.
  3. *7yo rule:* the ball must leave his hand before the receiver reaches the hoop.
  4. Throw from last week's phone-by-the-ear position.
  5. 6 to 8 throws, then swap roles (the 5yo can be a walking receiver for the 7yo).
- **Silent demo:** Point at the receiver and shake your head; point at the hoop and nod. Then make the throw while the receiver is still 2 steps away, and freeze, pointing at the hoop where the catch happens.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Throws at you (where you are now), so the ball lands behind you. **Fix:** Make the hoop the target with nobody there: 3 throws into an empty hoop, then add you walking. Point at the hoop, not at yourself.
  - *5yo:* Throws too hard and too high. **Fix:** Shorter distance. "Gentle rainbow to the hoop." Celebrate any throw that lands near it.
  - *7yo:* Waits until the receiver stops in the hoop (a "hook" habit). **Fix:** The release rule: only count catches where the ball was in the air before the receiver reached the hoop.
  - *7yo:* Leads too far and the ball sails past. **Fix:** Ask: "Where was he when you let go?" Then let him choose a later release point.
- **Make it fun:** Lands in the hoop or caught in it = touchdown (6). Within a step = field goal (3). The QB names each play ("Rocket Slant!").
- **Why it works:** Young QBs throw to where the receiver *is*. The hoop gives a fixed spot to aim at, which is much easier than predicting a moving target. That's an **external focus** and a simpler problem. As they improve, the moving receiver teaches timing on its own. This is **scaffolding**: a support (the hoop) you'll take away later.
- 🎥 **Video:** [Leading the receiver drill](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wIFpL-hGH8g) · *Verified title: "Passing Drill - Leading the Receiver" (Flag Football Academy).* A youth flag coach's drill with cone catch areas, the same idea as here. Also useful for team practices.

### Skill Work B: Ballhawk Breaks (6 min) · Cue: **Break**
- **Setup:** You are the QB with the football. The defender starts 5 steps in front of you, facing you. Two cones 4 steps to his left and right, slightly behind him: the places you'll throw.
- **Steps:**
  1. Defender in a ready stance: feet staggered, knees bent, chest slightly forward, eyes on your **throwing hand**.
  2. On "Hut!", he backpedals slowly while watching you.
  3. You make a throwing motion and throw (or lob) toward one cone.
  4. The moment your arm comes forward, he **plants and breaks** toward where the ball is going, at an angle, and tries to catch it or knock it down.
  5. *5yo:* walks backward instead of backpedaling. You lob a big foam ball gently toward a cone; he just has to get there and catch or touch it.
  6. *7yo:* real backpedal, real throws. Add pump fakes: he must not break on a fake.
  7. 6 to 8 reps each.
- **Silent demo:** You be the defender and let the 7yo be the QB. Backpedal with small steps, staring at his throwing hand. When his arm goes forward, plant your back foot, turn and burst toward the ball, catch it and hold it up. Then point at your eyes and at his throwing hand.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Turns around and runs away. **Fix:** Walk backward with him holding hands first. "Face the QB like he's the TV." Only 2 steps back.
  - *5yo:* Doesn't move until the ball lands. **Fix:** Lob higher and slower so he has time. Point at the ball in the air with your other hand.
  - *7yo:* Breaks too early (guesses) and you throw the other way. **Fix:** Pump fake. He'll learn fast that guessing gets punished. "Wait for the arm."
  - *7yo:* Rounds his break in a slow curve. **Fix:** "Plant and go." Put a cone where he should plant; he stops at it and bursts.
- **Make it fun:** He's a "ballhawk" swooping on the ball. Interception = 2 points, knockdown = 1. Catch it and he runs a "pick six" back past you.
- **Why it works:** Defense is about **reading a cue** (the QB's arm) and reacting, not memorizing. Pump fakes make him wait for the real signal, which trains **attention and impulse control**. It builds right on last week's backpedal, so you're adding one new thing (the break) to something he already knows.
- 🎥 **Video:** [Back-foot breaks](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_B7Cl0ein0A) · *Verified title: "Backfoot Breaks" (GridironStuds / All Eyes DB Camp - Chad Wilson).* A defensive-back coach explains breaking off the back foot from a backpedal. It's aimed at older players, so it's for you to understand the move, not to show the boys.

### Game: Pick-Six Showdown (6 min) · Cue: **Spot**
- **Setup:** You are the QB. One boy is the receiver, the other the defender. Two landing zones (hoops), one on each side, 5 to 8 steps away. The defender starts 4 steps off the receiver.
- **Steps:**
  1. The receiver secretly picks a landing zone (whispers it to you).
  2. On "Hut!", the receiver runs to his spot. The defender backpedals and tries to read where the ball is going.
  3. You throw to the spot, ahead of the receiver.
  4. Receiver catches = 6 points for him. Defender knocks it down = 2, intercepts = 6.
  5. Swap roles every 3 plays.
  6. *Handicaps:* when the 7yo defends his brother, he starts 6 steps away and can only knock the ball down, not intercept. When the 5yo defends, he starts just 2 steps away.
- **Silent demo:** Walk through one play in slow motion: point to the receiver and then his spot, mime the throw to the spot, then show the defender breaking on it. Finish by pointing at the spot where all three paths meet.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Forgets which spot he picked. **Fix:** Give the hoops names ("Moon" and "Star") so he can point instead of whisper.
  - *5yo:* As defender, chases the receiver instead of watching you. **Fix:** "Watch the QB's arm; it tells you where the ball goes." Throw a little slower to his side.
  - *7yo:* Runs to the spot at half speed. **Fix:** Throw it a step ahead. Slow receivers don't get there. The game teaches it.
  - *7yo:* As defender, grabs or pushes the receiver. **Fix:** Penalty: 6 points to the receiver. Say it calmly, once.
- **Make it fun:** Play-by-play commentary. "Pick six" interceptions get a celebration dance. Keep a running score in chalk on the driveway.
- **Why it works:** All three skills (lead pass, route, break) happen at once, so each boy learns **when** to use what he practiced. The secret spot means the defender has to read the QB, not just follow a pattern. That's **the game doing the teaching**, the main field-coaching idea for this week.

### Cool-down: Goalpost Stretch (3 min) · Cue: **Goalpost**
- **Setup:** Standing.
- **Steps:**
  1. Arms up in a goalpost shape (elbows bent at 90°, hands up). Slowly squeeze the shoulder blades back, hold 3 seconds, relax. 5 times.
  2. Slowly lean the goalpost to one side, then the other ("windy day"). 3 each way.
  3. Lie down for 3 slow breaths.
  4. Question: "When you were the ballhawk, what told you where the ball was going?"
- **Silent demo:** Make the goalpost shape, squeeze back with an exaggerated proud chest, then sway slowly like goalposts in a breeze.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Arms flop down. **Fix:** "The kicker is aiming, keep the posts up!" Hold your hands next to his as a guide.
  - *7yo:* Swings his arms fast. **Fix:** "The wind is gentle today." Count each hold slowly to 3.
- **Make it fun:** Once he's still, toss a small soft ball "through the uprights" (between his arms) as a last field goal.
- **Why it works:** It opens up the chest and shoulders after lots of throwing, and the question reinforces the key reading cue (the QB's arm) in his own words.

## 5. Session 4: Athleticism: Hop, Pattern, Blast Off (26 min)

**Equipment:** Sidewalk chalk (driveway) or a jump rope plus cones to mark squares on grass, 4 cones, a beanbag, a soft ball or stuffed toy ("the bacon"), a hat or towel. Sneakers on. Flat surface; soft grass for the lying-down starts.

**Levels this session:**
- **5yo:** Classic hopscotch (one foot on singles, two feet on doubles), one-foot hops on his stronger leg, with a hand to hold if he wants. Reaction starts from sitting, kneeling and lying on his tummy, reacting to something he can **see** (you drop a hat).
- **7yo:** Single-leg-only trips on both legs, then called patterns (left-left-right-right). Reaction starts from lying on his back and facing backward, reacting to a **sound**, with trick words.

> **Note on volume:** single-leg hopping is harder on young legs than it looks. Keep it to a few trips per leg per round with rest in between, stop when landings get loud or sloppy, and quit while it still looks good.

### Warm-up: Pogo Stick Bounces (5 min) · Cue: **Pogo**
- **Setup:** Flat grass or driveway. Two cones 6 to 8 steps apart.
- **Steps:**
  1. Small, quick bounces on both feet in place, on the balls of the feet, legs almost straight and springy like a pogo stick. 10 bounces.
  2. Pogo forward to the cone. Pogo backward to the start.
  3. Pogo side to side over a line.
  4. *7yo:* 5 pogos on one foot, then the other.
  5. *5yo:* 3 to 5 pogos on one foot with a hand on your arm.
  6. Short rests between. 2 rounds.
- **Silent demo:** Stand tall and bounce lightly on your toes, quick and small, arms relaxed, with a goofy grin. Then show a squishy, deep-knee bounce, frown and shake your head. Back to small springy ones and nod.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Big, deep squat jumps that tire him out. **Fix:** "Pogos are small and fast, like a bouncy ball." Bounce with him holding hands.
  - *5yo:* Lands flat-footed and loud. **Fix:** "Bounce on your tiptoes." Challenge him to bounce without his heels ever touching.
  - *7yo:* Heels slam down. **Fix:** Count only heel-free bounces.
  - *7yo:* Arms flail on the one-leg bounces. **Fix:** "Hold your pogo handles." Have him grip an imaginary pogo stick in front of his belly.
- **Make it fun:** Play a song and pogo to the beat. Shout "Pogo party!" and everyone bounces in place for 5 seconds.
- **Why it works:** Small, stiff, quick bounces train the **springiness** of the ankles and calves (the elastic part of running and jumping) and warm up the exact muscles needed for single-leg hops. Low effort, lots of contacts, safe at both ages.

### Skill Work A: Hopscotch Hustle (7 min) · Cue: **Spring**
- **Setup:** A hopscotch grid in chalk on the driveway, or made with a rope and cones on grass: about 8 squares in a line with some side-by-side pairs (the classic 1-2-1-2-1 layout). Each square about one big step.
- **Steps:**
  1. **Single squares = one foot. Double squares = two feet** (classic hopscotch).
  2. Hop through, turn on one foot at the end and come back.
  3. **Spring** means short, light, bouncy hops, landing on the ball of the foot with the knee slightly bent and pointing straight ahead.
  4. *5yo:* classic pattern, one-foot squares on his strong leg, holding your hand if he wants. 3 to 4 trips.
  5. *7yo:* single-leg-only trips (left leg one trip, right leg the next), then **called patterns**: you call "left-left-right-right" and he hops it. Toss a beanbag on a square; he hops over that square.
- **Silent demo:** Hop through the grid slowly with light, springy hops, arms out a little for balance. Freeze on one foot at the end, wobble, steady, turn and come back. Point to your knee lined up over your toes.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Puts his other foot down on single squares. **Fix:** Fine; he's 5. Count "one-foot squares made" and cheer each one. Hold his hand on the hard part.
  - *5yo:* Big leaps that overshoot squares. **Fix:** Smaller squares. "Little bunny hops, not kangaroo jumps."
  - *7yo:* Knee caves inward on landings (knock-kneed). **Fix:** "Knee points at your shoelace." Put one sticker on his knee and one on his shoe; they have to "look at each other" on every landing.
  - *7yo:* His weaker leg is much worse, so he avoids it. **Fix:** Start each round with the weak leg while he's fresh. Weak-leg trips count double.
- **Make it fun:** Classic hopscotch rules with the beanbag. For the 7yo, time a clean trip and try to beat it. Clean = no lines touched, no extra foot down.
- **Why it works:** Single-leg hops build **balance, ankle stiffness and leg strength on each side**, which feed sprinting, cutting and throwing (every throw finishes on one leg). Patterns add **thinking while moving**: he has to plan which foot comes next, the kind of mental-plus-physical work young athletes need. And hopscotch is a game kids already love, so the reps don't feel like training.
- 🎥 **Video:** [How to play hopscotch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Py51N-5WFPQ) · *Verified title: "How to Play Hopscotch - Equipment Free PE and Recess Games" (P.E. Pratt).* A PE teacher walks through the layout and rules. Handy for setting up the grid.

### Skill Work B: Wake-Up Starts (6 min) · Cue: **Blastoff**
- **Setup:** Two start spots side by side on soft grass and a finish cone 8 to 10 steps away for each boy.
- **Steps:**
  1. You call a start position and each boy gets into it:
     - Sitting cross-legged, facing the cone
     - Lying on the tummy, head toward the cone
     - Lying on the back
     - Kneeling
     - Standing **facing backward** (away from the cone)
  2. On the signal, he gets up as fast as he can and sprints to the cone. That's the **Blastoff**.
  3. *5yo signal:* you drop a hat or towel he can see. Positions: sitting, kneeling, tummy.
  4. *7yo signal:* you say "Blastoff!" Trick words like "Blast-cake!" or "Breakfast!" mean stay put. Positions: back, facing backward, tummy with eyes closed.
  5. 6 to 8 starts each, with a walk back between (that's the rest).
- **Silent demo:** Lie on your tummy, look around sneakily, then pop up with a quick push and step and sprint off. Do it from sitting too (roll onto a hand and knee, then up). Show the fast way up: hands push, one foot steps forward, go.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Stands up all the way, then starts running (two separate moves). **Fix:** Don't explain; race him gently from the same position. He'll copy your get-up.
  - *5yo:* Starts before the signal. **Fix:** "Rockets wait for the countdown." No penalty at 5, just a reset and a laugh.
  - *7yo:* Gets up slowly from his back. **Fix:** Ask: "What's the fastest way up from your back?" Let him experiment (rolling to the side and pushing up is usually fastest). Time each method.
  - *7yo:* Facing backward, he turns the long way and loses time. **Fix:** Let him test turning each way and time both. He picks the winner.
- **Make it fun:** For the 5yo, count down like a rocket launch ("3... 2... 1...") and drop the hat at a random moment. For the 7yo, the trick words are the fun, so make them silly.
- **Why it works:** Starts from awkward positions train **getting off the ground and accelerating**, which kids do all the time in real games after a fall, a dive or a slide. Trick words train **attention and inhibition** (not reacting to the wrong signal). Many different starting positions is **variable practice**, which builds an adaptable athlete rather than one who can only start from a set stance.
- 🎥 **Video:** [Quick Start](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NuHv4wDK14c) · *Verified title: "Quick Start" (Funetics).* A short kids' athletics activity using starts from different positions. Use it for ideas.

### Game: Wake-Up Bacon Snatch (5 min) · Cue: **Bacon**
- **Setup:** A soft ball or stuffed toy ("the bacon") in the middle. Each boy has a home line on opposite sides: 5 steps from the bacon for the 5yo, 6 to 7 steps for the 7yo. Flags on.
- **Steps:**
  1. Both boys get into a start position you call (sitting, lying, facing backward).
  2. You call "**Bacon!**" and they race to snatch the bacon and carry it back to their own home line.
  3. The one who doesn't get it can pull the snatcher's flag before he gets home.
  4. Snatch and make it home = 2 points. Pull the snatcher's flag = 1 point.
  5. *Handicap:* the 7yo starts farther away and in a harder position (on his back or facing backward); the 5yo can start sitting.
  6. Play to 7.
- **Silent demo:** Lie down, stare at the bacon, then on your own silent "go" leap up, snatch it and sprint back, holding it up like a trophy.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Grabs the bacon but freezes when chased. **Fix:** Play one round where you're the chaser at "grandpa speed," so he learns that running home works.
  - *5yo:* Gets upset losing to his brother. **Fix:** Bigger handicaps; aim for about even wins. Cheer his fast get-ups whoever wins.
  - *7yo:* Waits for his brother to grab it, then pulls his flag (a smart trick). **Fix:** Great thinking! If it makes the game boring, add a rule: you must touch the bacon within 3 seconds.
  - *7yo:* Pushes during the snatch. **Fix:** "Hands on the bacon, never on bodies." One warning, then a point to his brother.
- **Make it fun:** Sizzle sounds ("tsssss") while they wait. Sometimes call "Eggs!" or "Pancakes!" as fakes.
- **Why it works:** It chains a reaction start, an explosive first step, a quick change of direction and an escape into one game. Brothers love head-to-head contests, and the handicaps keep it close enough that both try their hardest.

### Cool-down: Snow Angel Breaths (3 min) · Cue: **Angel**
- **Setup:** Lie on your back on the grass.
- **Steps:**
  1. Slowly sweep arms and legs out wide (breathe in) and back together (breathe out), like making a snow angel.
  2. 6 slow angels.
  3. Hug both knees and rock gently side to side.
  4. Question: "Which wake-up position was the hardest to blast off from?"
- **Silent demo:** Lie down and make a slow, dreamy snow angel, eyes half closed, with a big slow breath.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Flails his arms fast. **Fix:** "Gentle angel, or the snow melts." Slow your own movement right beside him.
  - *7yo:* Wants to keep racing. **Fix:** "Champions cool down. Can you make the slowest angel ever?"
- **Make it fun:** Pretend it's snowing and narrate snowflakes landing on their noses.
- **Why it works:** Slow, wide movements and breathing bring the heart rate down and stretch hips and shoulders. The question makes them think about the skill, and their answer tells you which position to practice next time.

## 6. Session 5: Mixed Backyard Day: Halloween Haunted Gauntlet (28 min)

**Equipment:** Wiffle balls, a bat, a tee (optional), 2 orange balls or small pumpkins (or orange cones) as "pumpkin targets," a laundry basket on a chair, gloves, a soccer ball, a small goal, a foam football, a hula hoop, the hopscotch grid from Friday, a bedsheet and a chair (the "ghost"), a bucket and small "treat" tokens (wrapped candies, poker chips or bottle caps).

**Levels this session:**
- **5yo:** Hits off a tee or very short front toss toward the center pumpkin only. Gauntlet stations at the short distances, walking-speed fakes against the ghost.
- **7yo:** Front toss, calls his own pumpkin (center or opposite side). Gauntlet stations at full distance and speed.

### Warm-up: Monster Mash Moves (4 min) · Cue: **Monster**
- **Setup:** Open grass, two cones 15 steps apart.
- **Steps:**
  1. You call a monster and they travel between the cones moving like it:
     - **Zombie:** stiff-legged walk, arms out in front (stretches the back of the legs)
     - **Werewolf:** bear crawl on all fours
     - **Vampire:** side shuffle with a "cape" (arms out)
     - **Mummy:** high-knee march, arms straight out
     - **Frankenstein:** big lunging steps
  2. One trip each, then a final "Monster Dash" sprint.
  3. *7yo add-on:* on the second half of each trip, he does it backward.
- **Silent demo:** Do each monster for 3 seconds with an over-the-top face. They'll copy the face as much as the move, and that's fine.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Gets too silly and stops moving. **Fix:** Silly is good. Keep trips short and call the next monster quickly.
  - *5yo:* Can't bear-crawl the full way. **Fix:** Half the distance, then stand up and howl.
  - *7yo:* Rushes the lunges with tiny steps. **Fix:** "Frankenstein takes giant steps." Count steps: fewer is better.
  - *7yo:* Says it's babyish. **Fix:** Let him invent and lead one monster move. Ownership beats cool-factor.
- **Make it fun:** Monster sounds. Each boy invents a new monster for the last trip.
- **Why it works:** A **dynamic warm-up** (stretching while moving) gets the whole body ready, and the Halloween theme makes it fun enough that they don't notice it's a warm-up. Leading a monster gives the 7yo some ownership, a strong motivator at his age.

### Skill Work A: Pumpkin Patch Spray Hitting (7 min) · Cue: **Pumpkin**
- **Setup:** Home plate. Pumpkin targets: one straight up the middle (30 to 40 feet out) and, for the 7yo, one on the opposite-field side. Same safety setup as Session 1 (wiffle balls only, you off the line).
- **Steps:**
  1. Before each swing the hitter **calls his pumpkin** ("Center!" or "Other side!").
  2. *5yo:* hits off the tee or a very short front toss toward the center pumpkin only.
  3. *7yo:* front toss. When he calls the opposite side, you toss to the outside half.
  4. Ball rolls or flies within 2 big steps of the called pumpkin = 3 treat tokens. Right part of the field but not close = 1.
  5. Rounds of 5 swings, alternating brothers.
- **Silent demo:** Stand at the plate, point your bat at a pumpkin like Babe Ruth calling his shot, then swing and hold your finish with your chest facing that pumpkin.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Looks at the pumpkin instead of the ball during the swing. **Fix:** "Point at the pumpkin, then eyes go to the ball." Use the tee so there's no timing to worry about.
  - *5yo:* Forgets to call. **Fix:** You call it for him and point. Calling is a bonus, not essential at 5.
  - *7yo:* Calls the opposite side but pulls it anyway. **Fix:** Ask: "Where did you hit it, out front or a little deeper?" Remind him of Monday's gate. Toss a bit farther outside.
  - *7yo:* Gets angry when he misses a called shot. **Fix:** "Even big-leaguers miss their spot most of the time. Let's count how many you get." Normalize missing.
- **Make it fun:** The pumpkins are haunted. A ball near one "wakes it up" with a spooky sound effect from you.
- **Why it works:** It **reviews Monday's directional hitting** a few days later. Spacing practice out like this (rather than cramming) helps it stick. Calling the shot adds **intention**: he makes a plan and tests it, which makes him notice cause and effect.
- 🎥 **Video:** [Hitting to the opposite field](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pn3xtpCc8uM) · *Verified title: "Top 5 Ways to Teaching Hitting to the Opposite Field" (Dominate The Diamond).* Five youth-coach drills for opposite-field hitting, including "target practice" (calling a spot). For you, with ideas for the 7yo and your team.

### Skill Work B: Ghost Defender Finishing (6 min) · Cue: **Boo**
- **Setup:** A bedsheet draped over a chair (or a cone with a pillowcase) is the "ghost" defender, 8 steps from the start. A goal 5 steps behind the ghost.
- **Steps:**
  1. Dribble at the ghost.
  2. About 2 steps away, you whisper "**Boo!**" (or he says it himself). That's the moment to do his move: the Trick fake or the Slice from Tuesday.
  3. Get past the ghost and shoot.
  4. *5yo:* walking speed; any move counts, even just dribbling around the ghost.
  5. *7yo:* jogging speed, and he has to use a different move each rep (Trick, then Slice, then his own invention).
  6. 6 to 8 reps each.
- **Silent demo:** Dribble toward the ghost, mouth "Boo!" with a scared face, do a big fake, slip past and shoot. Then turn and wave bye-bye to the ghost.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Does his move too far away or right on top of the ghost. **Fix:** Put a "boo spot" (a leaf or small pumpkin) 2 steps before it.
  - *5yo:* Forgets to shoot after getting past. **Fix:** "Ghosts hate goals!" The goal is the reason to beat the ghost.
  - *7yo:* Does the move, then slows down and stops before shooting. **Fix:** "Get past, then quick shot." Count shots taken within 2 touches of beating the ghost.
  - *7yo:* Always uses the same move. **Fix:** That's why the rule says a different move each rep. Praise new ones loudly.
- **Make it fun:** The ghost "talks" in your spooky voice ("You can't get past meeee..."). A goal = 2 treats.
- **Why it works:** It repeats Tuesday's moves with a new look (a ghost instead of a troll). That's **variable practice**: same skill, different setting. "Boo" is a timing trigger. It tells him *when* to do the move, which is the hardest part of 1v1 for young kids.

### Game: Haunted House Gauntlet (8 min) · Cue: **Treat**
- **Setup:** A circuit of 4 stations, numbered with cones:
  1. **Hopscotch Crypt:** hop through Friday's grid (5yo classic pattern; 7yo single leg only).
  2. **Graveyard Grounder:** you roll a grounder; he fields it and throws to the basket at first (Tick-tock).
  3. **Ghost Pass:** he throws the foam football to the hoop ahead of the walking or jogging brother (or you).
  4. **Ghost Goal:** dribble past the ghost and shoot.
  - Each clean station = 1 **treat** token into the team bucket.
- **Steps:**
  1. Brothers take turns going through the whole circuit (about 40 seconds each).
  2. They're a **team** trying to fill the bucket to "the Monster's Number" (for example, 20 treats in 6 trips combined).
  3. Clean means done right, not fast.
  4. 5yo: short distances, big balls. 7yo: full distances.
- **Silent demo:** Walk the circuit once in slow motion, miming each station (hop, field-and-throw, pass, dribble-and-shoot) and dropping a pretend treat in the bucket after each. Hold up the bucket and shake it.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Gets lost between stations. **Fix:** Put a Halloween decoration or toy at each station. Walk with him the first time.
  - *5yo:* Upset when he misses a treat. **Fix:** It's team scoring: "Your brother's treats count for you too." Give a bonus treat for great effort.
  - *7yo:* Rushes and gets sloppy. **Fix:** "Only clean ones get a treat." Speed doesn't score.
  - *7yo:* Criticizes his brother. **Fix:** Make him the "treat giver" on his brother's trip: he drops in the treats and has to say one nice thing at each station.
- **Make it fun:** If they beat the Monster's Number, they keep the candy (or pick a reward). Halloween decorations and spooky music.
- **Why it works:** This is **interleaved practice**: switching between skills from different sports. It feels harder than repeating one skill, but it leads to better long-term learning. Team scoring keeps a 5-vs-7 contest out of it, and running the week's skills in a game-like order shows them how the pieces fit together.

### Cool-down: Jack-o'-Lantern Candle (3 min) · Cue: **Candle**
- **Setup:** Sit cross-legged facing each other.
- **Steps:**
  1. Each boy holds up one finger as the candle inside a jack-o'-lantern.
  2. Breathe in slowly through the nose ("smell the pumpkin pie").
  3. Breathe out slowly through the mouth so the candle **flickers but doesn't go out**: a long, soft breath.
  4. 5 slow breaths.
  5. Each boy names his best play of the week and the cue word that went with it.
  6. Finish with one specific piece of praise from you.
- **Silent demo:** Hold up your finger candle, take a big slow sniff, then blow out super gently while wiggling your finger like a flickering flame. Smile.
- **Errors & fixes:**
  - *5yo:* Blows hard ("I blew it out!"). **Fix:** Laugh, then "Can you make it flicker, not go out?" Show the soft breath again.
  - *7yo:* Can't pick a best play. **Fix:** Offer two: "The laser to the opposite field, or the pick six?"
- **Make it fun:** End by pretending to pinch out each candle with a "tssss" sound.
- **Why it works:** A long, slow breath out is the most calming part of breathing. Naming their best play and its cue word is **retrieval practice**: pulling the memory up is what makes it stick.

## 7. Coaching Groups at the Fields: New Tools for This Week

These build on what you already have (stations, kill the line, ask before tell, the Play, Practice, Play script). This week is about the "Play" parts: letting the game teach, and a few situations every volunteer coach runs into.

### A. Let the game do the teaching: running a short scrimmage
A short, small game teaches more than most drills, as long as it's set up right. Your job is game designer, not lecturer.
- **Make it small.** 2v2 or 3v3, not 9v9. Smaller games mean more touches per kid. In baseball, play a mini-game with 3 or 4 fielders, coach pitch, and 5-batter innings instead of a full game.
- **Make it short.** 3 to 4 minute rounds, then a 30-second water break and a rotation. Short rounds keep energy high and let you change one rule between rounds.
- **Use a rule that forces the skill.** That's a **constraint**: change the game and the kids change how they play. Examples:
  - Baseball (directional hitting): hits up the middle score 2 runs, everything else 1.
  - Baseball (field-and-fire): every out at first earns the defense a bonus point.
  - Soccer (1v1 moves): a goal only counts if the scorer beat a defender with a move first.
  - Flag football (lead passes): catches inside a cone zone are worth double.
- **Set it up, then step back.** Explain the rule in one sentence, start, and watch for a full 60 seconds before you say anything. Kids figure out much of what you would have told them.
- **Change the rule, not the kid.** If one team is losing badly, change the field size or add a handicap ("this team needs 3 passes before a shot") instead of stopping to coach.
- **Why it works:** Kids learn decisions (where, when, to whom) only in game-like settings. A rule that rewards the skill makes them *want* to use it, so motivation and learning come together.
- 🎥 **Video:** [Small-sided games that teach](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-sxiQzCWMWs) · *Verified title: "13 Small Sided Games for Youth Soccer Practice Sessions (SSG's) - Use Constraints to Teach" (Coach KW - 7v7 & 9v9 Soccer).* Soccer examples of small games with rule tweaks. The idea works in every sport.

### B. Coaching in-game with only cue words
During a scrimmage, long talk stops the game and loses the kids. Your tool is the cue word.
- **One word, one kid, one time.** "Laser, Mason!" (name + cue). Don't repeat it five times. Say it before the next rep, not after a mistake.
- **Use only words they learned in practice.** In-game cues work because they call up a picture from practice. A new word in the middle of a game is just noise.
- **Praise with the cue too.** "Great tick-tock, Ava!" tells the kid exactly what went right, and everyone else hears the cue again.
- **The 3-to-1 rule:** at least 3 positive cue-praises for every correction.
- **Save the full-team freeze for big moments.** At most once or twice in a 10-minute scrimmage, for 20 seconds: freeze, point, ask one question ("Where's the open space?"), restart. If you need to stop more than that, the game setup is the problem (go back to A).
- **Write the day's cue words on an index card** (or the back of your hand), and give the same card to parent helpers so everyone uses the same words.
- **Why it works:** Short cues can be processed while moving; longer explanations make the kid stop and think, which breaks the game. Pairing a cue with praise makes the right behavior more likely to come back.

### C. Team pregame warm-ups (a 15-minute routine)
A consistent pregame routine settles nerves, gets bodies ready and keeps 12 kids from climbing the dugout fence.
- **Same routine every game.** Kids this age love predictability. After 2 or 3 games they'll run most of it themselves.
- **A simple 15-minute plan (baseball):**
  1. **Minutes 0–4, moving warm-up:** a jog around the infield, then one line of dynamic moves (high knees, skips, shuffles, monster walks).
  2. **Minutes 4–9, throwing partners:** start short, back up slowly. Everyone throwing at once in pairs. No lines.
  3. **Minutes 9–13, grounders and flies:** two groups, each with a parent helper rolling grounders or tossing soft fly balls. Lots of quick reps.
  4. **Minutes 13–15, huddle:** one focus for the game ("Run hard through first base"), one team cheer, done.
- **Give each kid a job** (ball bag, cones, helmet check). Jobs reduce chaos.
- **Arrive with the plan written down** and brief helpers in the parking lot.
- **Don't teach anything new pregame.** It's warm-up and confidence, not practice.
- **Why it works:** Warm muscles are less prone to injury, a routine lowers anxiety, and starting with easy success (short throws, easy grounders) sends kids into the game feeling confident.

### D. Handling a tough parent
Most parents are great. Sooner or later one will be upset about playing time, positions or how you coached their kid.
- **Never in front of the kids, never during the game.** Say calmly: "I want to hear this. Can we talk right after practice?" or "Let's talk tomorrow." It buys time and takes the heat out.
- **The 24-hour rule:** ask parents to wait a day after a game before raising a concern. Share it at the start of the season ("Happy to talk about anything, after a cool-down day").
- **Listen first, fully.** Let them finish. Repeat back what you heard: "So you're worried Jake isn't getting time in the infield?" Feeling heard solves half the problem.
- **Explain your *why* in one or two sentences.** "Everyone rotates through all positions at this age. Jake plays infield in the next two games." Keep it about the team plan, not the kid's flaws.
- **Agree on one next step.** "Let's check in after next week's game."
- **If it gets heated or personal, end it politely** ("Let's bring the league coordinator into this") and loop in the league. You're a volunteer; you don't have to take abuse.
- **Prevent it:** a 2-minute start-of-season talk or email explaining rotations, playing time and how to raise concerns heads off most problems.
- **Why it works:** Most conflict comes from a parent feeling unheard or surprised. Clear expectations up front and calm listening defuse both.

### E. Keeping score when "we don't keep score"
In tee ball and coach pitch, many leagues "don't keep score." The kids keep it anyway, and every one of them knows who won.
- **Don't pretend.** If a kid asks "Who won?", it's fine to say "Officially we don't keep score at your age. What did *you* do well today?" Don't deny a score they all know.
- **Keep different scores.** Count the things you want more of, out loud, for your team: hustle plays, runs through first, good throws, cheers for teammates. "We made 8 great throws to first today!"
- **Team vs. a target, not team vs. team.** In practice, "Can we get 10 outs at first this round?" is a scoreboard with no losers.
- **Handle the gloating kid quickly.** "Good teams win nicely. What do we say to the other team?" Then line up for handshakes.
- **Handle the crushed kid privately.** "That one felt bad to lose. What was your best play?" Then move on. No lecture.
- **At home, do keep score in backyard games.** Brothers learning to win and lose gracefully in low-stakes games is great practice for the real thing.
- **Why it works:** Kids naturally compare, and ignoring it confuses them. Changing *what* you count steers their attention to effort and skill, the things they control.

### F. A rule-tweak menu (constraints you can pull out any time)
Keep this on an index card in your bag. When a scrimmage isn't working, pick one:
- **Too easy for one side:** shrink the field for them, give the other team an extra player, or require 3 passes before scoring.
- **Everyone bunches around the ball:** add a second ball, or mark zones each kid must stay in.
- **Nobody uses the new skill:** make it worth double points.
- **Too many stoppages:** coach pitch only (no walks), 5-batter innings, or "continuous" play (no stopping between plays).
- **Energy dropping:** shorten rounds to 2 minutes and race the clock.
- **Why it works:** Each tweak changes the problem the kids are solving, which changes how they play, with no lecture needed.
