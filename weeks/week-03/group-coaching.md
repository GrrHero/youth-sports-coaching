# Week 3 · Coaching Groups at the Fields: New Tools for This Week

[Week 3 overview](README.md)

These build on the basics you already have (freeze signal, stations, tears, positive language).

## A. Kill the line
Lines are where attention and behavior fall apart. If you have 10 kids in one line, each kid gets about 1 rep every 2 minutes and spends the rest bored, poking the kid in front.
- **Rule of thumb: no more than 2 or 3 kids waiting per ball.** If you have 12 kids, run 4 groups of 3, or have everyone do the drill in pairs at once.
- **One ball per pair, not one ball per coach.** For grounders: kids pair up 15 feet apart and roll grounders to each other (Chomp drill). You walk between pairs and coach.
- **Give the waiting kid a job:** counter, ball-feeder, "umpire," or "next up practice" (doing Popcorn in place while waiting).
- **Count reps, not minutes.** At the end of a drill ask: "How many grounders did each of you get?" If the answer is under 10, the drill was too line-heavy.

## B. Bat safety spacing (the "Bat Bubble")
Swinging bats are the biggest injury risk at youth baseball practice.
- **One bat out per hitting station.** All other bats lie flat in the dugout or a marked "bat bed." Kids may *only* pick up a bat at the station.
- **Mark the hitting zone** with a hula hoop or 4 cones. Only the hitter stands inside. The next hitter waits *behind a cone at least 3 big steps back and off to the side*, not behind the hitter's back.
- **Check before you swing:** teach every hitter to hold the bat out at arm's length and turn slowly in a full circle before swinging ("Bubble check"). If the bat touches anything, someone's too close.
- **Space hitting stations far apart** (at least 20 to 25 feet), all **facing the same direction** toward a fence or net, so no one is ever in another station's swing path or hit path.
- **Coach positions:** when you toss, stand at a front diagonal (not straight ahead) behind an L-screen or out of the line of the ball when possible. Use soft balls for front toss with young kids.
- **"Drop it, don't throw it."** Teach a bat drop after contact from day one, and practice it as part of baserunning drills.

## C. Pairing mixed ages and skill levels
- **Big Buddy / Little Buddy** for teaching moments: an older or stronger kid feeds balls or demonstrates for a younger one. It gives the advanced kid a role and responsibility, not boredom.
- **Similar-level pairs** for competitive games: kids learn most when the challenge is close. A strong kid crushing a weak one teaches neither.
- **Challenge by choice:** set out cones at 3 distances (near, middle, far) and let each kid pick. Most kids choose well, and those who don't will move after a few misses. This handles the skill range without singling anyone out.
- **Handicap rules** for mixed pairs: the stronger player uses the weak hand, walks instead of runs, or needs 3 catches to the other's 1. (Just like the Treasure Keeper brother rules.)
- **Switch pairs every 10 to 15 minutes** so cliques don't form and nobody gets stuck with a mismatch.

## D. "Ask before tell"
Instead of giving the correction, ask a question that lets the kid find it.
- **Instead of:** "Get your glove down." **Ask:** "Where was your glove when the ball went under?"
- **Instead of:** "Step toward your target." **Ask:** "Where were your toes pointing on that throw?"
- **Instead of:** "Run through the bag." **Ask:** "Where's the finish line on first base?"
- **Then:** "Show me." Let them try again right away.
- Keep questions short and about **one thing**. If they don't know, offer two choices: "Was your glove up or down?"
- Why it works: kids remember what they figure out themselves much better than what they're told. Each question takes 5 seconds, so it doesn't eat practice time.

## E. The kid who won't participate
Common at 5 to 7: they're shy, scared, tired, overwhelmed, or upset about something else.
- **Don't force or bargain in front of everyone.** It turns into a standoff.
- **Give a job first:** "Can you be my ball counter?" or "Can you hold the cones for me?" Being *near* the activity with a role is step one.
- **Offer a tiny entry:** "Want to do just 2 grounders with me, just you and me?" Small, private, low-risk.
- **Invite, then walk away.** "We'd love you in the next round when you're ready." Then don't watch them. Kids often join once the pressure is off.
- **Pair them with a kind buddy** rather than putting them in front of the group.
- **Check in privately** later ("Was something hard today?"), and mention it to the parent calmly and without blame if it's a pattern.

## F. Working with parent helpers
Parents want to help but often over-coach or coach the wrong thing.
- **Give each helper ONE job and ONE cue word.** For example: "You run the grounder station. The only cue word is Chomp. Count each kid's reps." A short index card helps.
- **Brief them for 2 minutes before practice**, not during: what the station is, the cue, and "praise effort, give one correction at a time, then let them try again."
- **Ask for "no coaching from the fence."** Give fence parents a fun job instead (cheering for effort, timing runs).
- **Rotate kids, not helpers.** Helpers stay at their station and get better at running it.
- **Thank them in front of the kids.** It builds a team culture.

## G. A quick 3-part practice script (45 to 60 minutes)
Use "**Play, Practice, Play.**"
1. **Play (10 to 15 min):** Kids arrive and go straight into a game (e.g., Pounce, Grounder Goalie, a mini game). No lines, no lecture. Late arrivals just join in.
2. **Practice (20 to 25 min):** 2 or 3 short stations focused on **one theme** (e.g., "ground balls and throwing"). 6 to 8 minutes per station, one cue word per station, ask-before-tell coaching. Kids rotate on your freeze signal.
3. **Play (15 to 20 min):** A real game or game-like scrimmage using the theme. Let the game do the teaching and coach only with quick cues. End with a 1-minute huddle: "What did we work on?" plus one specific praise for the group.
