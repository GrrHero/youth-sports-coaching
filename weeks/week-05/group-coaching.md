# Week 5 · Coaching Groups at the Fields: New Tools for This Week

[Week 5 overview](README.md)

Fall ball wraps up around Nov 14, so this week is about finishing well: fair playing time, smarter defense, the kid who's scared of the ball, a backup plan for rain or early darkness, and ending the season so every kid wants to come back. These build on what you already have (stations, kill the line, ask before tell, let the game teach, cue words in games).

## A. Fair playing time and rotating positions (a simple rotation chart)
In 8U, the fastest way to lose a kid (and a parent) is a season of right field. A written rotation solves most of it before it starts.
- **Write it before game day.** A simple grid: kids down the side, innings across the top, a position in each box. Bring it on an index card and give a copy to your assistant. Deciding in the dugout leads to favorites without meaning to.
- **Rules that keep it fair:**
  - Nobody sits two innings in a row, and nobody sits twice before everyone has sat once.
  - Every kid plays at least one **infield** inning per game (or per two games if you have a big roster).
  - Nobody plays the same position two innings in a row (except maybe a catcher, if your league uses one).
  - Track it across games: over the season, every kid should see pitcher's helper, first base, the left side of the infield and the outfield.
- **First base is the one exception to think about.** It's the busiest spot in coach pitch and a kid who's scared of the ball can get hurt there. Rotate kids into first base only after they've caught throws in practice (Session 1's Stretch & Squeeze is perfect for this). Be open about why: "You'll play first once we've practiced it together."
- **Batting order rotates too.** Move the top of the order down 3 spots each game, so no kid always bats last.
- **Say it out loud early:** "Everyone plays everywhere on this team. That's how you get better at this age." Kids and parents accept rotations when they know the rule and see it applied to everyone, including the coach's kids.
- **Why it works:** Kids at this age haven't settled into positions, and the ones who look weakest now are often the late bloomers. Playing everywhere builds all-round skills (and game sense), and a visible, written plan takes the "favorites" worry off the table.

## B. Teaching game situations with a 2-minute walk-through ("Where's the play?")
Most 8U defensive mistakes aren't catching or throwing; they're not knowing where to throw. Teach it walking, not talking.
- **Freeze-frame walk-through:** put the kids at their positions, a parent or coach as a runner on a base, and say: "Runner on first. Ball hit to you, [position]. Where's the play?" Then walk it in slow motion: the fielder carries the ball (no throwing) to where it should go, and everyone else walks to where *they* go (covering a base, backing up).
- **Keep it to 3 situations per practice:** for 8U, the big three are:
  1. **Nobody on:** throw to first. Always.
  2. **Ball in the outfield:** throw it in to the cutoff/the base in front of the lead runner. Run it in if in doubt ("Get it to an infielder fast").
  3. **Runner on first, ground ball to an infielder:** the closest out. Often that's stepping on second or tagging the runner if he's near; otherwise throw to first.
- **Use a pre-pitch question.** Before each pitch in practice games, call out: "Where's the play?" and have the kids point to the base. It takes 2 seconds and builds the habit of thinking *before* the ball is hit.
- **Make the walk-through a game:** "Situation Freeze": you call a situation and count down from 5; everyone has to be in the right spot when you hit zero.
- **One cue word for the whole team:** "**Lead runner**." (Only add it after the "throw to first" habit is solid.)
- **Why it works:** Walking it slowly removes the time pressure so kids can see the whole picture, the same whole-part-whole idea as at home. Pointing before each pitch is **anticipation practice**, which is what experienced players do automatically.

## C. The kid who's afraid of the ball
Every 8U team has a few. They step away from the plate, turn their head on throws or hide in the outfield. Fear is normal and smart (a baseball *can* hurt). Your job is to make the ball less scary, not to talk them out of it.
- **Never say "Don't be scared" or "It won't hurt."** Both make them think about getting hurt. Say instead: "Let's start with a ball that can't hurt at all."
- **Go back to safe balls.** Foam, tennis or "safety" balls for catching and hitting. Let the kid pick when to move to the next ball. Progress: foam → tennis → soft-core → real ball, closer distance first, then back up.
- **Teach how to get hit safely.** For hitters: practice "turn and tuck" with a foam ball, turning the front shoulder and chin away from an inside pitch toward the catcher. Once they *know* how to protect themselves, they relax. For fielders: "glove in front of your face" catches with a soft ball.
- **Shrink the distance and slow the speed.** Make catches easy enough that they succeed 8 out of 10 times. Confidence comes from success, not courage.
- **Fix the feet, not the fear, at the plate.** A kid stepping "in the bucket" (away from the plate) often just needs a target: a line or a bat on the ground pointing to the pitcher to step on.
- **Pair them with patient partners** in throwing pairs, not with the hardest thrower.
- **Praise brave efforts, not results.** "You kept your eyes on it all the way into the glove!" even when the ball pops out.
- **Talk to the parent privately.** Ask what the kid has said at home. Sometimes it's one bad memory (a ball off the face last spring). Share your plan so they don't push at home.
- **Why it works:** Gradual exposure with lots of success is how fear goes down. Teaching a protective skill gives the kid a sense of **control**, and kids who feel in control stop flinching.

## D. Rainy-day and after-dark practice plan (indoor or covered)
With daylight saving time over and a few November storms, you'll lose a practice or two. Have a plan ready so you can say "practice is on, but here's where" instead of cancelling.
- **Where:** a school gym, church hall, covered pavilion, a big garage, or even a parent's driveway with lights. Ask your league; many have a gym slot or a covered batting cage for rain-outs.
- **Equipment swap:** foam or wiffle balls only indoors, tees instead of live pitching, and no full swings near walls or ceilings (use "Bat Bubble" spacing). Tape lines and bases on the floor.
- **A 45-minute indoor plan (4 stations, 8 minutes each, plus warm-up and finish):**
  1. **Warm-up (5 min):** Four-Corner Sport Switch from Session 5 (baseball moves only) or a "situations freeze" walk-through.
  2. **Station 1, Tee hitting into a net or a gym mat:** wiffle balls, aim at a target taped on the wall.
  3. **Station 2, Short-hop grounders and Field-and-Fire:** parent rolls; kids field and throw to a parent at a taped first base with tennis balls.
  4. **Station 3, First base Stretch & Squeeze:** soft tosses to a taped base.
  5. **Station 4, Baserunning with tape:** run through first (Tape from Week 3), make a turn, and practice the Glove-Down Tag with a parent "runner" walking in.
  6. **Finish (5 min):** a quick team game like "Situation Freeze" or a relay, plus the huddle.
- **After-dark option:** if you only have 30 minutes of light, cut to two big stations and the game, and end by sunset. Kids can't track fly balls in dusk; skip fly balls entirely late in the day.
- **Have a short "at home" message ready** for a full cancellation: "Practice is off. 10-minute homework: 25 throws into a laundry basket and 20 tee swings. Tell me tomorrow how many went in!" Kids love homework that's a challenge.
- **Why it works:** Kids at this age need consistency more than perfect conditions. A short indoor practice keeps the routine and the team energy going, and stations keep everyone busy in a small space.

## E. Planning the last practice and the end-of-season celebration
The last practice is the one they'll remember. Make it fun first, skills second, and make sure every kid leaves feeling seen.
- **Last practice = "Fun Day":** let the kids vote on their two favorite drills from the season, then play a kids vs. coaches & parents game (parents bat opposite-handed, run backward to first, etc.). End with a long huddle.
- **Kids coach for 5 minutes:** let a couple of volunteers teach a drill to the team using a cue word they learned ("Coach's word?" from Week 2). They'll remember teaching more than anything else.
- **Awards that every kid earns, for character, not stats.** Give each player a specific award: "Hustle Hero" (ran out every hit), "Best Teammate" (cheered loudest), "Iron Glove" (stopped everything at third), "Most Improved Hitter," "Brave Heart" (kept trying when it was hard). Write one sentence for each kid about a real moment from the season. The specific sentence matters more than the certificate.
- **Avoid MVP or ranking awards** at this age. One "MVP" makes 11 kids feel they weren't.
- **Keep the celebration simple:** a snack at the field after the last game, a team photo, and the award moments. Ask one parent to organize food so you can focus on the kids.
- **Team culture to finish with:** a team cheer the kids made up, everyone signs a ball for the coach or each kid gets a ball signed by the team, and a goodbye handshake line where each kid says one thing they liked.
- **Why it works:** Ending on a strong positive memory is what makes kids want to play again next season (the "peak-end" effect: people judge an experience mostly by its best moment and its ending). Character awards reinforce the behaviors you've praised all season, effort and teamwork, rather than talent.

## F. End-of-season conversations (with kids, parents and helpers)
A few short conversations at the end turn a good season into a lasting one, and set you up for spring.
- **With each kid (1 minute, during the last practice or game):** name one specific thing they got better at ("Remember when you couldn't catch a grounder? Today you made three outs at short."), and one thing to try this winter ("Play catch with your mom or dad 10 times a week"). Kneel to their eye level. That 60 seconds is often what the kid remembers most.
- **Wrap-up message to parents (short email or team-app post):**
  1. Thank them, especially the helpers by job (not by name if posting publicly).
  2. Two or three team highlights ("We made our first double play!").
  3. What the kids learned: rotations, cue words, effort over results.
  4. A simple off-season idea: play catch, hit off a tee, try another sport.
  5. Spring registration info, if you have it.
- **Thank your helpers privately**, maybe with a small card signed by the kids. Ask them if they'd help again.
- **Ask for feedback** with one or two questions ("What's one thing your kid loved? What's one thing I could do better next season?"). Read it later with a cup of coffee, not right after the last game.
- **Handle the "will my kid be on your team next season?" question gently:** "I'd love to coach them again. Team assignments are up to the league, but I'll mention it."
- **Write yourself a "next season" note** while it's fresh: what worked (stations, cue words), what didn't, which parents helped. Future-you will thank you.
- **Why it works:** Specific, personal feedback at the end tells each kid their effort was noticed, which is the strongest motivator for keeping kids in sports. And a clear, positive wrap-up keeps parents on your side for next season.
