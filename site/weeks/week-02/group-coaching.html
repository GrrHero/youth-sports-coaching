Week 2 · Group coaching at the baseball fields: new topics for Week 2

Week 2 overview

📺 Watch first: Top 10 Tee Ball (& "Coach Pitch") Coaching Tips — YouGoProBaseball. Two coaches talk honestly about feeling lost coaching 5–7-year-olds for the first time, plus practical tips like where to stand at the tee.

1. First-practice routines

What to do: - Arrive 15 min early with cones already set up. The first kid who shows up gets a job ("Can you help me put balls in the bucket?") so early arrivals have something to do besides chaos. - Use name tape on helmets or shirts. Learning names on day one is the biggest step toward building trust. - Use a 3-part opener every practice: (1) circle and names, (2) the same warm-up game every time, (3) "Today we're learning ___." Kids this age feel safe when they know what happens next. - Teach the field with a game: "Run to first base! Now run to the pitcher's mound!" It works as a warm-up and teaches the field at the same time. - Keep the first practice under 60 minutes, and spend no more than 10 minutes on any one drill.

Script: "Hi, I'm Coach Greg. My job is to help you have fun and get better. Your job is to try hard and be a good teammate. Deal? Shake on it!" Why it works: A predictable routine means you only need to show kids how practice works once. After that, the structure does the work and you spend less time managing behavior.

2. Parent helper management

What to do: - Give every helper a job card (an index card with one station, one cue word, and one rule). Example: "Tee Station. Cue: DOT. Rule: Every kid gets 5 swings, then rotates." - Recruit with specific, small asks: "Could you run the throwing station for 10 minutes?" works much better than "Can anyone help?" - Have a 2-minute parent huddle before practice: today's cue words, the freeze signal, and "praise effort, one tip max." - Stop sideline coaching kindly and early: "We're working on just one thing today. Can you cheer for effort? It helps them so much." - Thank helpers by name in front of the kids at the end.

Why it works: Parents want to help but don't know how, and a job card answers that. It also keeps the message the same at every station, so kids don't hear five different coaches saying different things.

3. Keeping fielders busy in tee ball and coach pitch games

What to do: - Ready-position game: Before every pitch, call "Ready!" Fielders slap their gloves and get in the "sumo" stance. Give a point to every kid who's ready. - "Where's the play?" Before each batter, ask the fielders "If the ball comes to you, where do you throw it?" They yell "First!" - "Ball buddies" rule: Pair each fielder with a neighbor. On any hit, both buddies run to the ball, and one fields while the other backs them up. This stops the 9-kid dogpile. - Rotate positions every inning. Nobody plays outfield twice in a row. - Have outfielders do mini jobs: count pitches, shout the outs, or play "statue" between batters.

Why it works: Bored kids drift (dirt digging, dandelion picking). Giving them a small job on every pitch keeps their attention on the game without you nagging.

4. The kid who won't participate

What to do: - Don't force it, and don't ignore it. Make him your "assistant coach": he holds the bucket, says "Go!", or counts swings. Being near the activity comes before doing it. - Offer a small first step: "Can you try just one throw to me? Then you can go back to being my helper." - Find the reason quietly: scared of the ball, tired, overwhelmed by the group, or new and shy. A softer ball, a smaller group, or a buddy fixes most of these. - Check in with the parent privately and positively: "He's warming up to it. What does he love at home?" Use that in your coaching ("Throw like a dinosaur!"). - Praise any participation, quietly, not in front of the whole team.

Why it works: Taking small steps toward the activity, with nothing bad happening, builds courage. Big public praise can make a shy kid hide.

5. The kid who dominates

What to do: - Give him a leadership job instead of shutting him down: line leader, equipment captain, or "demo kid" for one drill. Leaders still have to wait their turn. - Add a challenge just for him: "You hit it off the tee every time, so now try to hit it to the left side," or "Catch it backhand." - Use rules that share the ball: "The ball has to be touched by 2 different players before the throw to first." - Praise teamwork out loud: "Did you see how he let his buddy field that? That's a big-league teammate." - Talk privately if he's bossing others: "You're really good. Good players help others get good too. Can you help me with that?"

Why it works: Kids who take over usually want attention or a bigger challenge. Giving them a job and something harder to try uses that energy instead of fighting it, and keeps the other kids in the game.

6. Ending practice well

What to do: - End with the most fun game, not a drill or a lecture. Examples: "Coach Pitch Home Run Derby" (one swing each, run all the bases), or "Pick-Up Race" (who can put the most balls in the bucket). - Do a 60-second huddle: "What was your favorite thing today?" (Take 2–3 answers.) Then "Next time, we'll ___." - Do a team cheer with hands in. - Hand each kid off to a parent with one good thing: "She stepped on every throw today!" Parents remember this, and kids hear it again at home. - Have the kids help with cleanup: "Everyone grab 2 cones!"

Why it works: Kids remember how practice ended most. A good ending makes them want to come back, which matters more than any drill.

7. Game-day dugout management

What to do: - Use a lineup card taped up in the dugout with names and pictures or colors for non-readers. Assign one "on deck" parent to help. - Set up a helmet station: helmets lined up in batting order on the bench or a fence. "Batter, on deck, in the hole" is three kids, all with helmets on. - Make a bat-safety zone: bats stay in the bat bag or rack. Nobody touches a bat until they're on deck. Draw a line in the dirt if you need to. - Use a dugout parent whose only job is to keep kids seated, hand out water, and track the lineup. - Give dugout jobs: cheer leader (leads one team cheer per inning), glove buddy (hands gloves out when it's time to field), out counter. - Run fast transitions: "When I yell 'Gloves on!', run to your position. Let's see if we can beat 30 seconds!" - Keep snacks and water routines simple: water bottles labeled and lined up; snacks only after the game.

Why it works: Dugouts are where things fall apart (lost helmets, swinging bats, crying over turns). Clear jobs and a set routine keep kids safe and the game moving, and every kid gets a moment to be in charge.
