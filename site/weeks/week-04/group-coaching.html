Week 4 · Coaching Groups at the Fields: New Tools for This Week

Week 4 overview

These build on what you already have (stations, kill the line, ask before tell, the Play, Practice, Play script). This week is about the "Play" parts: letting the game teach, and a few situations every volunteer coach runs into.

A. Let the game do the teaching: running a short scrimmage

A short, small game teaches more than most drills, as long as it's set up right. Your job is game designer, not lecturer. - Make it small. 2v2 or 3v3, not 9v9. Smaller games mean more touches per kid. In baseball, play a mini-game with 3 or 4 fielders, coach pitch, and 5-batter innings instead of a full game. - Make it short. 3 to 4 minute rounds, then a 30-second water break and a rotation. Short rounds keep energy high and let you change one rule between rounds. - Use a rule that forces the skill. That's a constraint: change the game and the kids change how they play. Examples: - Baseball (directional hitting): hits up the middle score 2 runs, everything else 1. - Baseball (field-and-fire): every out at first earns the defense a bonus point. - Soccer (1v1 moves): a goal only counts if the scorer beat a defender with a move first. - Flag football (lead passes): catches inside a cone zone are worth double. - Set it up, then step back. Explain the rule in one sentence, start, and watch for a full 60 seconds before you say anything. Kids figure out much of what you would have told them. - Change the rule, not the kid. If one team is losing badly, change the field size or add a handicap ("this team needs 3 passes before a shot") instead of stopping to coach. - Why it works: Kids learn decisions (where, when, to whom) only in game-like settings. A rule that rewards the skill makes them want to use it, so motivation and learning come together. - 🎥 Video: Small-sided games that teach · Verified title: "13 Small Sided Games for Youth Soccer Practice Sessions (SSG's) - Use Constraints to Teach" (Coach KW - 7v7 & 9v9 Soccer). B. Coaching in-game with only cue words

During a scrimmage, long talk stops the game and loses the kids. Your tool is the cue word. - One word, one kid, one time. "Laser, Mason!" (name + cue). Don't repeat it five times. Say it before the next rep, not after a mistake. - Use only words they learned in practice. In-game cues work because they call up a picture from practice. A new word in the middle of a game is just noise. - Praise with the cue too. "Great tick-tock, Ava!" tells the kid exactly what went right, and everyone else hears the cue again. - The 3-to-1 rule: at least 3 positive cue-praises for every correction. - Save the full-team freeze for big moments. At most once or twice in a 10-minute scrimmage, for 20 seconds: freeze, point, ask one question ("Where's the open space?"), restart. If you need to stop more than that, the game setup is the problem (go back to A). - Write the day's cue words on an index card (or the back of your hand), and give the same card to parent helpers so everyone uses the same words. - Why it works: Short cues can be processed while moving; longer explanations make the kid stop and think, which breaks the game. Pairing a cue with praise makes the right behavior more likely to come back.

C. Team pregame warm-ups (a 15-minute routine)

A consistent pregame routine settles nerves, gets bodies ready and keeps 12 kids from climbing the dugout fence. - Same routine every game. Kids this age love predictability. After 2 or 3 games they'll run most of it themselves. - A simple 15-minute plan (baseball): 1. Minutes 0–4, moving warm-up: a jog around the infield, then one line of dynamic moves (high knees, skips, shuffles, monster walks). 2. Minutes 4–9, throwing partners: start short, back up slowly. Everyone throwing at once in pairs. No lines. 3. Minutes 9–13, grounders and flies: two groups, each with a parent helper rolling grounders or tossing soft fly balls. Lots of quick reps. 4. Minutes 13–15, huddle: one focus for the game ("Run hard through first base"), one team cheer, done. - Give each kid a job (ball bag, cones, helmet check). Jobs reduce chaos. - Arrive with the plan written down and brief helpers in the parking lot. - Don't teach anything new pregame. It's warm-up and confidence, not practice. - Why it works: Warm muscles are less prone to injury, a routine lowers anxiety, and starting with easy success (short throws, easy grounders) sends kids into the game feeling confident.

D. Handling a tough parent

Most parents are great. Sooner or later one will be upset about playing time, positions or how you coached their kid. - Never in front of the kids, never during the game. Say calmly: "I want to hear this. Can we talk right after practice?" or "Let's talk tomorrow." It buys time and takes the heat out. - The 24-hour rule: ask parents to wait a day after a game before raising a concern. Share it at the start of the season ("Happy to talk about anything, after a cool-down day"). - Listen first, fully. Let them finish. Repeat back what you heard: "So you're worried Jake isn't getting time in the infield?" Feeling heard solves half the problem. - Explain your why in one or two sentences. "Everyone rotates through all positions at this age. Jake plays infield in the next two games." Keep it about the team plan, not the kid's flaws. - Agree on one next step. "Let's check in after next week's game." - If it gets heated or personal, end it politely ("Let's bring the league coordinator into this") and loop in the league. You're a volunteer; you don't have to take abuse. - Prevent it: a 2-minute start-of-season talk or email explaining rotations, playing time and how to raise concerns heads off most problems. - Why it works: Most conflict comes from a parent feeling unheard or surprised. Clear expectations up front and calm listening defuse both.

E. Keeping score when "we don't keep score"

In tee ball and coach pitch, many leagues "don't keep score." The kids keep it anyway, and every one of them knows who won. - Don't pretend. If a kid asks "Who won?", it's fine to say "Officially we don't keep score at your age. What did you do well today?" Don't deny a score they all know. - Keep different scores. Count the things you want more of, out loud, for your team: hustle plays, runs through first, good throws, cheers for teammates. "We made 8 great throws to first today!" - Team vs. a target, not team vs. team. In practice, "Can we get 10 outs at first this round?" is a scoreboard with no losers. - Handle the gloating kid quickly. "Good teams win nicely. What do we say to the other team?" Then line up for handshakes. - Handle the crushed kid privately. "That one felt bad to lose. What was your best play?" Then move on. No lecture. - At home, do keep score in backyard games. Brothers learning to win and lose gracefully in low-stakes games is great practice for the real thing. - Why it works: Kids naturally compare, and ignoring it confuses them. Changing what you count steers their attention to effort and skill, the things they control.

F. A rule-tweak menu (constraints you can pull out any time)

Keep this on an index card in your bag. When a scrimmage isn't working, pick one: - Too easy for one side: shrink the field for them, give the other team an extra player, or require 3 passes before scoring. - Everyone bunches around the ball: add a second ball, or mark zones each kid must stay in. - Nobody uses the new skill: make it worth double points. - Too many stoppages: coach pitch only (no walks), 5-batter innings, or "continuous" play (no stopping between plays). - Energy dropping: shorten rounds to 2 minutes and race the clock. - Why it works: Each tweak changes the problem the kids are solving, which changes how they play, with no lecture needed.
